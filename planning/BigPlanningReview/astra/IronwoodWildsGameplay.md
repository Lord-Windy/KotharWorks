# Ironwood Wilds — gameplay brief

A household steps through a crossing into a sparsely settled frontier. Across
the water stands a coppice; above it, a seam of workable iron. The household has
a pick, an axe and a letter asking for supplies. Home is under attack. The
people pressing that war may be sending out expeditions much like yours. The
letter needn't settle who is right, and the next expedition needn't receive the
same letter.

**You direct a household, one person at a time.** Mara can be mining while Ivo
walks home. Each person takes one timed action and can hold three more orders. A
return journey can be queued behind a three-minute job. When the orders run out,
that person waits. Logging off neither freezes a running job nor gives anyone
permission to choose new work. This makes the household equally legible from a
row of browser tabs or to a program deciding whom to direct next.

## What keeps a household busy?

- **Work has a place.** Ore in Mara's pack on the ridge cannot be shipped from
  the landing. Ground, distance, carrying space and people's time matter. The
  text should tell you enough to make the decision: “two units left in this
  face; a full load is a 45-second walk from home.”
- **Practice belongs to a person; technology belongs to the household.** A miner
  gets faster through repeated mining. Bringing the first timber home unlocks
  *Timber Framing* and a choice of what to build. Eventually framing and masonry
  together might unlock a gatehouse, while an experienced builder can finish one
  sooner. Losing a specialist hurts without erasing what the household knows.
- **Home offers relief marks for deliveries.** Spend marks to recruit another
  person, whether that's an extra worker or a replacement for someone lost. Keep
  earned marks in the chronicle even after they're spent: “how much did we
  send?” and “what can we afford?” are different questions.
- **A hold is a project with a use.** The first room in the proposed slice is a
  packing shed: it makes a load of ore worth shipping at a better rate. Later,
  stores, workshops and walls turn a homestead into a castle. Space, protected
  stock and work capacity give each addition a purpose. The interesting grind is
  choosing who to train and what to build while another person is busy.
- **Expeditions and raids have different stakes.** Later, you can explicitly
  send a party of your own people against monsters for materials or access to a
  site. Party members can die. At the hold you set a standing defence plan, such
  as which store to protect first. An NPC raid can take exposed goods; it cannot
  kill residents. Other households cannot attack, steal from or order yours. For
  a household with no survivors and too few marks, I'd allow one unskilled
  relief arrival after a cooldown while that condition holds. Losing a party
  should make a story, not end the account.

## What makes this a simulation worth watching?

Two timber can buy a third worker early or become the shed that earns more on
future shipments. A longer walk to the ore can change which plan is worthwhile.
A later community project could shorten that walk for everyone: even a household
that never ordered a miner onto the road would see its options change. Those are
consequences of shared rules, not scripted plot beats. A good chronicle entry
names the cause: “The first packed ore paid for Linn's passage.”

The proposed unit of play is a **season**: one seeded frontier with its own
households, technology, stores, people and marks. A normal server reboot resumes
that frontier. A deliberate season reset archives its story and starts a new
expedition; accounts and published chronicles survive. The home-world war can
supply a different demand each season without needing a canonical ending.

The [first expedition](FirstExpedition.md) exercises the economic choice with
two people and three places. Castles, monster parties and hold raids come after
there is a household worth protecting.
